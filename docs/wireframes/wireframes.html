Climate policy and the limits of carbon pricing

Carbon pricing has become the default instrument of climate policy across advanced economies. It is widely recognised that market-based mechanisms offer a cost-effective pathway towards decarbonisation while preserving economic flexibility. Furthermore, such approaches align incentives across a diverse range of stakeholders. Yet the Australian experience between 2012 and 2014 suggests that political durability matters more than theoretical efficiency.

The scheme covered roughly 60 per cent of national emissions and applied a fixed price of $23 per tonne, rising annually before a planned transition to a floating price — a design that critics on both sides found reasons to oppose. In my view the repeal was not a verdict on carbon pricing itself but on the coalition that built it.

Emissions in the electricity sector fell by around 7 per cent during the fixed-price period, though attribution remains contested. …
